# The Decentralization of Practice

## Definition
A philosophical approach to skill development and life in general that emphasizes diversification and adaptability over specialization. It suggests that focusing solely on a single area of expertise creates vulnerability and limits potential for growth, while a broader, more diffused approach fosters resilience and opens up new possibilities.

## Explanation
Ido’s assertion that one should "decentralize" their practice, and that specializing too much creates vulnerability, is a powerful critique of modern approaches to skill acquisition and personal development.  It's a rejection of the "all eggs in one basket" mentality.  If your identity and worth are tied to a single skill or achievement (like the one-arm handstand), any setback or change in circumstances can be devastating. Decentralization, in contrast, involves cultivating a range of skills and interests, so that if one area falters, others can compensate. This isn't about mediocrity; it's about building a foundation of versatility that allows for adaptation and resilience.  It echoes the concept of a polymath – someone with expertise in many different fields – and suggests that a broader skillset is not a distraction from mastery, but a prerequisite for it.  This principle extends beyond physical movement to encompass all aspects of life, from career to relationships.

## Key Insight
True mastery isn't about specializing in a single area, but about cultivating a diverse skillset and a flexible mindset that allows for adaptation and resilience.

## Examples
* **Movement:** A movement practitioner who focuses solely on handstands is more vulnerable to injury or plateaus than one who cultivates a broader range of movement skills.
* **Career:** A professional who relies solely on a single skill is more susceptible to job loss or industry disruption than one with a diverse skillset.
* **Finance:** Diversifying investments mitigates risk and increases the potential for long-term growth.

This concept isn't advocating for superficiality. It’s about strategic diversification – identifying core skills and then expanding outward to related areas. The key is to maintain a balance between depth and breadth.
