# The Parasitic Nature of Explicit Knowledge

## Definition
This concept posits that explicit knowledge – the kind we learn through instruction and language – often obscures or replaces direct sensory experience and intuitive understanding of the world. It suggests that language, while useful, can be a "parasite" that invades and distorts our perception of reality.

## Explanation
The speaker argues that language and explicit instruction, while valuable tools, can also create a barrier between us and direct experience. By relying on verbal explanations and conceptual frameworks, we risk losing the ability to perceive and understand the world through our senses and intuition. This isn't a rejection of language altogether, but a caution against its dominance. True understanding, he suggests, arises from direct engagement and observation, unmediated by intellectual constructs. This framework highlights the importance of cultivating sensory awareness and challenging the assumptions embedded in our language and thought patterns. It connects to broader philosophical discussions about the limitations of representation and the primacy of experience.

## Key Insight
Explicit knowledge, while useful, can obscure direct sensory experience and hinder our ability to perceive reality as it is.

## Examples
* **Text Example:** "It's all the time replacing the the sensing of the world, the recognition and observation of reality."
* **Outside Example:** A beginner guitarist might focus on learning scales and chords, losing sight of the feeling and flow of playing music.

## Notes
This concept is nuanced and easily misunderstood. It’s not arguing that language is inherently bad, but that we must be mindful of its potential to distort our perception and prioritize direct experience.
